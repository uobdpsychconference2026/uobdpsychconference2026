Who's in the Driver's Seat: Exploring Self-Knowledge in the Age of Tech
Dr. Chasity O'Connell
Professor of Practice, American University of Sharjah
Clinical Director, Thrive Centre for Wellbeing, Dubai

We are in an age of something unprecedented and almost paradoxical; our algorithms seem to know us better than we know ourselves. They have mapped our patterns, know what we like, anticipate our needs and wants, and actively shape what appears on our screens (often without us noticing).

Now, I am not an Tech Scientist or AI researcher. However, I am interested in how AI and technology are impacting us in terms of our development and well-being. I've seen clients and students rattle off sophisticated concepts in psychology and mental health. However, when it comes to understanding or experiencing that concept, there is a gap. However, when it comes to collecting information vs understanding a concept, there is a gap. Now, educational institutions try to help us move from knowing to understanding for major content areas, but there really isn't a curriculum for understanding yourself. After all, we are in the "information age" (ironically, not the "knowledge age").

This talk will explore ways to deepen and interact with our own self-knowledge and self-awareness. Using emotional intelligence as a framework, we will explore what emotional intelligence is, how it helps us build self-knowledge and self-awareness as we engage with technology. While we may fantasize about life before smartphones, social media and AI, we are past the point of turning back. And so, the idea is to help us feel a deeper sense of self-fortification so that we can attempt to maintain our spot in the driver's seat when driving these tech highways.

Exploring the Impact of Maladaptive Religious Schemata on Depression and Anxiety in a Muslim Student Sample
Taregh Shaban, PhD1; Ian Grey, PhD2; Craig Steel, PhD3
1. School of Psychology, University of Birmingham Dubai
2. Department of Cognitive Sciences, United Arab Emirates University
3. University of Oxford

Depression and anxiety are major concerns among university students in the GCC, where reported rates exceed global averages despite high levels of religiosity, which paradoxically, are often associated with better mental health. This study examined whether culturally shaped misinterpretations of religious teachings - conceptualised as maladaptive religious schemata (MRS) - are associated with elevated depression and anxiety scores among Muslim students.

To investigate whether childhood and adulthood MRS increase the likelihood of meeting clinical cut-offs for depression and anxiety, and whether particular religious subschemata show distinct relationships with student mental health.

A cross-sectional sample of 250 undergraduate students at a public university in the GCC completed the Child and Adult Religious Schema Questionnaires for Culturally Distorted Beliefs (C-ReSQue-CD and A-ReSQue-CD), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7. Logistic regression analyses examined associations between high ReSQue-CD scores and clinically significant symptoms while controlling for gender and religiosity.

Students with high C-ReSQue-CD or A-ReSQue-CD scores were approximately three times more likely to meet the clinical cut-off for depression. High childhood and adulthood ReSQue-CD scores also increased the likelihood of meeting the anxiety cut-off, with patterns varying by developmental stage.

The findings suggest that student mental health assessments should distinguish supportive religious beliefs from culturally distorted interpretations. Culturally sensitive, developmentally informed interventions may help clinicians and university wellbeing services address religiously framed guilt, fear, shame, and anxiety without undermining students’ faith commitments.

Complex yet integrated: The well-being of Third Culture Kids (TCKs) in the multicultural context
Magdalena Mosanya, PhD1
1. Murdoch University Dubai

Background: Social changes of the twenty-first century increased the number of people who grow up "between cultures" in "mobile" families. This research project deals with the broadly understood well-being of multicultural individuals defined as third culture kids (TCKs) who spent a significant part of their developmental years in cultures different from their parents' cultures . The features that all TCKs have in common are international mobility (transience), permanent contact with cultural diversity, and the need to negotiate personal, social, and cultural identity in response to changing cultural contexts (cultural homelessness). Aims: The research project comprises three studies. Two studies aimed to analyse the relationships between the broadly understood well-being of TCKs in the UAE with multicultural identity configurations, including psychosocial mediators. The third study explored the sociocultural adaptation and acculturative stress of TCKs in relation to three groups of factors: individual, social, and cultural. Results: We showed that TCKs' well-being depends on internal integration versus identity compartmentalisation and categorisation, not mere exposure to diversity or international mobility. We confirmed the hypotheses about the enhancing role of global mindset, cultural intelligence, and social support. Conclusion: This project significantly deepened our understanding of how a TCK’s identity interconnects with their overall well-being and social functioning, highlighting the vital resources (i.e. multicultural identity integration) that contribute to their thriving.

“Do You Understand My Culture?” Broaching Culture and Mental Wellbeing Among Emirati Youth
Saeed Alhemeiri1; Mustafa Aydogan, PhD1
1. Zayed University

Mental health care is strengthened when clients feel that their identities, values, and sociocultural contexts are recognized within the therapeutic relationship. Broaching refers to counselors’ intentional efforts to acknowledge and explore how culture, identity, and sociopolitical context shape clients’ experiences and concerns (Day-Vines et al., 2007). Recent broaching literature further emphasizes openness, cultural humility, and willingness to engage cultural differences rather than avoid them (Aydogan et al., 2026; Depauw et al., 2025). This is particularly relevant in the UAE, where Emirati youth may receive care from providers trained within diverse and often Western-influenced mental health frameworks.

This ongoing qualitative study explores Emirati youths’ experiences of cultural broaching in therapy. Preliminary analysis of three semi-structured interviews suggests four emerging themes: (a) “Ask, Don’t Assume,” reflecting preference for therapists who approach culture with curiosity; (b) “How You Ask Matters,” emphasizing timing, permission, and sensitivity when addressing cultural topics; (c) “Shared Culture Does Not Guarantee Feeling Understood,” indicating that cultural similarity alone does not ensure culturally responsive care; and (d) “Feeling Understood Opens the Door,” reflecting how cultural responsiveness may influence trust, disclosure, withholding, and continued engagement. Additional interviews and analysis are ongoing, and further findings will be presented at the conference.

By centering Emirati youth voices, the study highlights the importance of understanding wellbeing within ongoing cultural and social transitions. Findings may inform culturally responsive interventions, broaching skills, provider training, professional standards, licensing procedures, and mental health policy in the UAE.

Beyond the Western model: how Black British communities cope with mental distress, and what it teaches us about culturally responsive support for students
Obajide Alademerin1,2; Siân Lowri Griffiths3; Luke Brown1,3
1. University of Birmingham
2. Reliable Routes Psychological Services Ltd
3. Institute for Mental Health, University of Birmingham

Student mental health support in UK institutions is largely built on Western, statutory models of care that overlook the cultural and community frameworks through which many students understand and manage distress. This is especially consequential for Black students, who face disproportionate mental health inequalities yet remain underserved by conventional provision.

This study explored how Black British communities recognise, manage and cope with mental distress. Using a constructionist qualitative design, we conducted nine online focus groups with 36 adults (aged 23–68), community members, mental health professionals and third-sector practitioners, and analysed the data using reflexive thematic analysis.

Five interconnected coping responses emerged: mental resilience (emotional suppression as adaptive survival); faith in distress (church and prayer as primary support); and escapism through substances. Notably, two further themes were distinctly generational and novel: physical and creative release — exercise, music and poetry as therapeutic outlets and increasingly open peer support, with younger people, and young men in particular, moving from activity-based companionship toward direct emotional dialogue. These emerging, strengths-based practices signal a cultural shift among exactly the age group entering higher education.

I will draw out the implications for student mental health: how universities might embed culturally responsive support that builds on faith, creative expression and peer connection rather than replacing them, and how belonging and early intervention could be reimagined for Black and minoritised students. The talk argues for prevention co-developed with communities, not imported onto them.

Social Anxiety and Student Wellbeing: Understanding Pathways to Social Disconnection and Loneliness
Rabea Aftab1
1. Bath Spa University RAK Campus, Ras Al Khaimah, UAE

Social anxiety is a prevalent and often overlooked challenge among young adults and university students. Characterised by fears of negative evaluation and rejection, social anxiety can affect how young people engage with others, form relationships, and experience belonging, with wider consequences for student wellbeing, social connectedness, and loneliness. The present study aimed to explore how social anxiety may be associated with loneliness among university students, with particular attention to the interpersonal experiences that may contribute to this relationship. Data were collected from 400 university students in the UAE.Standardised measures were used to assess social anxiety, interpersonal problems, and loneliness. A mediation analysis using the PROCESS macro was conducted to examine whether interpersonal problems mediated the relationship between social anxiety and loneliness. The findings showed a significant relationship between social anxiety and loneliness, with interpersonal problems emerging as a significant mediator of this association. These findings highlight that the impact of social anxiety may extend beyond individual psychological distress to the quality of young people’s social relationships and their experience of connectedness. The findings suggest that loneliness should not be addressed solely as an isolated wellbeing concern; understanding the interpersonal difficulties underlying social disconnection may be critical to effective prevention and intervention. For universities and student support services, this highlights the importance of moving beyond symptom-focused approaches towards interventions that strengthen social confidence, interpersonal functioning, meaningful social connection, and belonging. Addressing these relational pathways may provide a more holistic approach to redefining student mental health and wellbeing.

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence technologies into everyday life has transformed the way university students learn, communicate, and interact with others. While AI tools offer significant educational and practical benefits, concerns have emerged regarding the potential consequences of excessive reliance on these technologies. This study aims to elucidate the relationship between generative AI overuse, identity formation, and interpersonal behaviors among university students. AI as Support or Stressor: Artificial Intelligence, Student Mental Health, and Higher Education
Saima Nomaan1
1. University of Birmingham Dubai

Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming increasingly embedded in university students’ learning, help-seeking, and everyday digital lives, creating new possibilities for supporting mental health while introducing new psychological risks. This presentation examines AI's dual role as both a support and a stressor for student mental health and well-being in higher education. Drawing on a systematic review of 40 studies published between January 2023 and August 2025, identified through Scopus, ERIC, APA PsycINFO, and PubMed, the presentation synthesises evidence across AI-enabled chatbots, generative AI, adaptive learning tools, wearables, emotion-recognition systems, and predictive analytics. The findings indicate that AI-supported interventions can reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression, strengthen self-efficacy and problem-solving, enhance perceived social support, and support engagement and emotional well-being. However, these benefits coexist with important risks. Intensive or poorly regulated AI use may contribute to technostress, digital fatigue, emotional dependence, privacy concerns, algorithmic bias, and reduced human connection. Outcomes are also shaped by AI literacy, self-regulation, patterns of use, cultural attitudes towards help-seeking, and institutional policies and safeguards. The presentation argues that AI should not be understood simply as beneficial or harmful to student well-being. Its psychological effects depend on how, why, and within what institutional conditions it is used. Universities therefore need human-centred approaches that position AI as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, human support, while embedding governance, digital well-being practices, inclusive design, and appropriate pathways to professional support. These findings offer practical implications for educators, counsellors, policymakers, and institutions navigating AI-enabled student support.

Measuring the Wrong Variable: What a Sedentary-Behaviour App Can and Cannot Tell Us About Student Wellbeing
Hamid Mukhtar, PhD1
1. University of Birmingham Dubai

Digital wellbeing tools face a delivery problem, not an efficacy problem. Mental health applications show real efficacy in controlled trials, yet usage data across 93 popular apps found a median 15-day retention of 3.9%. Whether an intervention is ever received is largely determined by design: a layer psychology has ceded to engineering.

This presentation draws on Sedentaware, a mobile system that detects prolonged sedentary behaviour and delivers adaptive feedback calibrated to each user's progress, and on a published framework mapping persuasion strategies onto motivation, ability and trigger. I present the prototypes that failed before one succeeded, and the behavioural reason for each: passive tracking raised awareness without changing behaviour; scheduled reminders produced habituation and abandonment. Persuasive mechanisms are not interchangeable, and choosing between them is a psychological problem first.

I then argue that such systems measure the wrong construct for wellbeing purposes. Prospective evidence indicates that mentally passive sedentary behaviour elevates depression risk (RR = 1.17), whereas mentally active sedentary behaviour does not (RR = 0.98). Current sensing systems, including my own, cannot distinguish them, and so interrupt studying as readily as disengaged scrolling.

I close with a feasibility study I am developing: passive sedentary sensing combined with brief context prompts that serve as both measure and training label, in a student population. Such an instrument could reach students who never present to services. Its central obstacles are not technical: defining the construct, validating the inferences, and setting the limits of consented monitoring are questions for psychology.

From Helicopter Parenting to Attitude towards Psychological Help-Seeking: A Predictive Model Among Malaysian Undergraduates
Surabhi Roy1; Kristy Chong, PhD2; Shue Ling Chong, PhD1,3
1. University of Nottingham Malaysia
2. Monash University Malaysia
3. University of Nottingham Ningbo China

First-year undergraduates living away from home are particularly vulnerable to mental distress, yet psychological service utilization remains low. As of 2020, over 50% of university students in Malaysia struggle with stress and depression, and over 70% have reported anxiety, with these statistics continuing to rise. Therefore, understanding help-seeking in this expanding and vulnerable population is increasingly urgent. Grounded in the Self-Determination Theory, this study developed and tested a predictive model examining the influence of familial and academic factors on attitude towards seeking psychological help. Structural equation modelling was used to analyze data collected from 101 first-year undergraduates at the University of Nottingham, Malaysia. Results indicated, among students living away from home, helicopter parenting positively predicted perceived parental support and negatively predicted academic achievement. Perceived parental support positively predicted academic achievement and competence satisfaction. Additionally, competence satisfaction predicted a positive attitude towards psychological help-seeking. These findings highlight competence satisfaction as a key pathway linking familial and academic factors to help-seeking, with implications for early intervention among at-risk university students in collectivist cultural contexts.

Teacher Burnout in the UAE: Exploring Burnout, Wellbeing and Proactive Coping
Tanya McGuane1,2
1. Heriot-Watt University Dubai
2. Caya World Clinic, Dubai

Teacher burnout has emerged as a significant global occupational health concern, contributing to declining workforce wellbeing, reduced job satisfaction, and increasing teacher attrition. These concerns are relevant within high-performance educational systems such as the United Arab Emirates (UAE), where demands may negatively impact educator mental health and workforce sustainability. This study investigated psychological burnout and proactive coping among public-school teachers in the UAE, with focus on gender differences and the potential protective role of proactive coping in wellbeing. A quantitative cross-sectional design was employed using data collected from 171 public-school teachers across the UAE. Participants completed the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and the Proactive Coping Inventory (PCI). Statistical analysis revealed significantly elevated burnout levels across the sample, with male teachers reporting significantly higher burnout scores than female teachers (p = .025). This finding is noteworthy as it contrasts with common assumptions surrounding emotional strain within teaching professions and may reflect the psychosocial pressures experienced by male expatriate educators in demanding international educational environments. Occupational workload, cultural expectations, relocation-related stress, and provider-role pressures may contribute to increased vulnerability within this cohort. No statistically significant gender differences were identified in proactive coping strategies. The findings highlight the growing psychological burden experienced by educators within demanding educational environments and reinforce the importance of preventative wellbeing initiatives within educational systems. The study contributes region-specific insight into burnout in the Middle East and identifies the need for institutional approaches that support resilience, stress management, and educator wellbeing to promote sustainable human performance and workforce retention.

Supporting Students Under Strain: Staff Resources, Professional Identity and Wellbeing During Regional Conflict
Gary Pheiffer, PhD1
1. Heriot-Watt University Dubai

Regional conflict places university staff in a dual position: managing uncertainty themselves while supporting students and maintaining educational continuity. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and an identity-motive perspective, this study examined how conflict affected staff resources, professional identity and capacity to support student wellbeing in UAE higher education.

Twenty-five teaching and research faculty and professional administrative staff participated in semi-structured interviews. Data were analysed using thematic analysis, informed by COR concepts of resource loss, resource caravans and resource caravan passageways, alongside identity motives including efficacy, meaning, belonging and continuity.

Some initial themes were: First, uncertainty depleted staff members’ emotional and cognitive resources while increasing demands for reassurance, flexibility and guidance. Second, supporting students became identity-relevant: it protected professional meaning and efficacy but intensified strain when staff lacked information, authority or resources. Third, faculty and professional staff acted as resources for students by interpreting communications, adapting academic or administrative processes, providing emotional support and connecting students with assistance. Finally, institutional conditions shaped this resource transfer. Timely communication, role clarity, managerial support and flexibility created resource caravan passageways that enabled staff to assist students; ambiguity and workload pressure constrained their capacity.

The findings demonstrate that student wellbeing depends partly on the resources and wellbeing of those providing support. Universities should protect staff capacity through credible communication, manageable workloads, role clarity, managerial support and effective referral pathways. Sustaining staff resources strengthens institutional support for student mental health and wellbeing during conflict.

Imposter Syndrome among Undergraduates: Examining the Roles of Field of Study and Gender
Ishan Misra, Ayesha Sarwar (Co-Author)
Department of Psychology, Middlesex University Dubai

Objective: Imposter syndrome (IS) is an internal experience wherein individuals consistently undermine their own abilities and deem themselves as fraudulent, despite apparent evidence of their accomplishments. Undergraduate students have shown vulnerability to IS, with women consistently reporting higher IS levels than men due to various reasons. However, prior research studies are limited in their generalizability due to excessive focus on STEMM undergraduates, with a dearth of research on IS in students of other fields. This study examined whether a student's field of study, their gender, and the interaction significantly predicted IS levels among undergraduate students in the United Arab Emirates.

Methods: A between-subjects factorial design was utilized, with students residing in and pursuing an undergraduate degree in the UAE being recruited via convenience and snowball sampling. A survey link containing the relevant questionnaires along with consent and debriefing information was shared, and a total of 161 participants' data was analyzed.

Results: Findings indicated that IS characteristics were prevalent in the sample, with a high number of students experiencing frequent IS feelings. The analysis provided inconclusive differences in IS between students of different fields and the interaction between field of study and gender. Conversely, gender significantly influenced IS levels, with female students reporting higher IS than males.

Conclusions: These findings highlight the widespread prevalence of IS among undergraduate students, even across disciplines. They also suggest the increased importance of gender in shaping IS experience among UAE undergraduates. Limitations, strengths, and directions for future studies were discussed to provide a foundational structure.

Does the Medium Matter? University Students' Preferences for Text- and Voice-Based Conversational AI in Digital Mental Health Support
Parvathii Renjith

Conversational artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly explored as digital mental health support, potentially addressing economic, systemic, and cultural barriers of traditional services. As AI-based support grows among young people, understanding how students experience different interaction modalities is essential. Existing research has examined text- and voice-based conversational AI separately, with comparative research largely quantitative and Western. This highlights a gap in student experiences within culturally diverse settings such as the UAE, where rapid AI adoption intersects with mental health needs.

This qualitative study explored university students' perception and preferences for text- versus voice-based conversational AI for digital mental health support, in collaboration with Mentra, a UAE-based AI mental health chatbot. Eleven University of Birmingham Dubai students observed standardised fictional interactions between an individual experiencing Generalized Anxiety Disorder and an AI chatbot in text and voice formats. Semi-structured interviews were analysed using Braun and Clarke's (2019) Reflexive Thematic Analysis.

Three themes emerged: Overall Experiences and Impressions of AI Chatbot Interaction; Contextual Factors Shaping Format Selection; and Format-Specific Attributes Influencing Preference. Participants valued AI's accessibility, convenience, and non-judgemental support, but perceived limited depth and human connection, viewing it as a complement rather than replacement for human support. Text was preferred for privacy, reflection, and task-focused support, though interactions could feel rigid. Voice was more emotionally engaging and convenient for multitasking, but raised privacy concerns. Preferences were dynamic, shaped by individual, situational, and modality features, highlighting the need for accessible, flexible, culturally responsive, and safe AI support aligned with diverse student needs.

Hyper Independence as a Barrier to Seeking Psychological Help Among Working Female University Students
Nishwa Tistic

Hyper independence, the tendency to avoid relying on others even when support is genuinely needed, has been suggested as a psychological barrier to help-seeking, but there's still limited research testing this directly, especially among working women juggling both academic and job responsibilities. This study looked at the relationship between hyper independence and self-stigma around seeking psychological help in working female university students. Participants completed the Hyper Independence Questionnaire (HIQ) and the Self-Stigma of Seeking Help Scale (SSOSH). The results showed a significant positive correlation between hyper independence and self-stigma, r = .503, p < .001, with hyper independence explaining about 25.3% of the variance in self-stigma (R² = .253). In other words, students who scored higher on hyper independence also tended to report more discomfort around the idea of seeking psychological support, even though nothing suggests they were dealing with less distress than their peers.

These findings matter for student mental health and wellbeing because they suggest reluctance to seek help isn't always about access or awareness. Sometimes it comes down to how deeply someone has internalized the idea that needing support means weakness, especially for students balancing financial independence, academic pressure, and social expectations all at once. This points to a real need for university counseling services to treat self-reliance itself as a barrier worth addressing, not just the practical obstacles that stand between students and support.

Evaluating the Effectiveness of Virtual Reality Systematic Desensitization for Public Speaking Anxiety
Dr. Rupa Kalahasthi, Alisar Kaysoun, Kawthar El Chediak
Rochester Institute of Technology, Dubai

Public speaking anxiety (PSA) is one of the most prevalent forms of anxiety and associated with significant academic, occupational and social impairment. Systematic exposure therapy with VR has proven to be an effective treatment for anxiety disorders; however, there are limited studies to date that have investigated the effect of the length of time spent on individualized Virtual Reality Systematic Desensitization (VRSD) on treatment effectiveness. The purpose of this study is to assess the effectiveness of a 4-session VRSD intervention as compared to a separate 8-session VRSD study with similar intervention and evaluation procedures.

Methods: Participants with self-reported public speaking anxiety will be randomly divided into two groups — one receiving the 4-session intervention and the other receiving the 8-session intervention. The pre- and post- Public Speaking Anxiety Scale (PSAS), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS) and biological measures of heart rate, skin conductance and body temperature will be compared. The VRSD protocol includes a personalized fear hierarchy, virtual reality exposure, relaxation, and biofeedback. Both interventions use the same VR scenarios at the same intensity.

Hypothesis: It is hypothesized that both protocols will result in the same amount of reduction in PSA symptoms as measured by PSAS, GAD-7 and SUDS.

Conclusion: A comparison of outcomes between intervention durations may offer a preliminary guide to the importance of treatment duration for VRSD of PSA, and inform the creation of personally tailored VR-based interventions for university students.

Digital Wellbeing in the Social Media Era: Understanding Social Media Detoxification in Adults
Sherine Bou Dargham (The Valens Clinic, King's College London), Lara Daouk (The Valens Clinic), Rama Kanj (The Valens Clinic, University of Reading)
The Valens Clinic

Social media has become an increasingly prominent part of everyday life, and concerns surrounding its problematic use and implications for mental health have become an important area of research. Problematic use has been associated with lower self-esteem, depressive symptoms, and sleep disturbance. In response, social media detoxification (SMD), defined as a temporary break from social media, has emerged as a strategy for promoting healthier digital engagement. However, evidence regarding its effects on wellbeing remains mixed.

In a previous study, we developed a scale to explore the characteristics of SMD in university students. At the time, this was one of the earliest attempts to systematically investigate SMD among university students. Our findings indicated considerable variability among those who engaged in SMD in terms of detox duration, perceived improvements, platforms abstained from, and gender. This is consistent with recent research, with some studies reporting small but positive effects of SMD on wellbeing, while others suggest that it may have adverse effects.

Here, we revisit our original findings in the context of this expanding evidence and provide an overview of what is currently known about SMD, its potential benefits and limitations, and factors that may influence its effectiveness. Building on this work, we propose a larger-scale investigation of SMD among university students, examining individual differences, motivations, and patterns of detoxification. We will also introduce a complementary questionnaire under development examining parents' awareness and perception of technology and social media use among children, extending our research into digital wellbeing across developmental stages.

The Influence of Emotion Regulation Strategies on Obsessive-Compulsive Symptom Dimensions Among University Students
Eden Elsa Luis, Maliha Masood (Co-author), Dr. Anita Shrivastava Kashi (Co-author)
Middlesex University Dubai

Obsessive-compulsive Symptom Dimensions (OCSD) are severely distressing and prevalent in non-clinical populations. A noteworthy factor contributing to OCSD is Emotional Regulation (ER) and its strategies, cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression. Previous research has examined ER strategies in clinical, situational, or experimental settings; less is known about how trait-level tendencies toward ER strategies relate to OCSD in non-clinical university student populations. The present study hypothesized that ER would significantly predict OCSD of contamination, responsibility for harm and mistakes, symmetry/incompleteness, and unacceptable thoughts. A quantitative, within-group study design was adopted. Three hundred and one largely Asian university students aged 18 years and above were recruited through convenience sampling. Emotional regulation was assessed with the ER Questionnaire, and OCSD were measured with the Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale. Four multiple regressions were conducted to analyse the impact of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression on OCSD. Cognitive reappraisal emerged as a significant negative predictor of all OCSD, while expressive suppression was a positive predictor of contamination only. Expressive suppression did not significantly predict responsibility for harm and mistakes, symmetry/incompleteness, or unacceptable thoughts. The results suggest that ER strategies may have a stable, significant impact on OCSD characteristics when embedded as trait-like tendencies. It assists professionals in forming interventions for university students to engage in adaptive ER. Recommendations for future research include the formulation of OCD-specific ER measures for university student populations and accounting for cultural differences as a demographic variable.

Emotion Regulation and Healthcare-Seeking and Healthcare-Avoidance Behaviors Among Undergraduate Students in the United Arab Emirates
Areeba Ilyas, Anushka Naomi Daulagala, Ala Achour, Efthymios Papatzikis
Canadian University Dubai, Dubai, United Arab Emirates

Healthcare-seeking and healthcare-avoidance behaviors are important to understanding how students engage with healthcare. Whether students seek care or avoid it bears directly on whether those experiencing distress reach support, making these behaviors relevant to student mental health and wellbeing. In this study, an exploratory analysis found a small positive association between healthcare-seeking and healthcare-avoidance behaviors (Spearman's rho = .208, p = .035), indicating that they do not represent opposite ends of a single dimension. This finding is consistent with the separate healthcare-seeking and healthcare-avoidance dimensions reported by the developers of the HCbQ.

This study examined whether emotion regulation strategies, specifically cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression, were associated with healthcare-seeking and healthcare-avoidance behaviors among undergraduate students in the United Arab Emirates. Using a cross-sectional correlational design, 103 undergraduate students aged 18 or older were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling across UAE universities. Participants completed the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) and the Healthcare Behaviors Questionnaire (HCbQ). The study was reviewed and approved by the Canadian University Dubai Human Subjects Research Ethics Committee prior to data collection.

Contrary to the hypotheses, neither expressive suppression nor cognitive reappraisal was significantly associated with healthcare-seeking or healthcare-avoidance behaviors (all p > .29). Emotion regulation variables explained minimal variance in these behaviors (R-squared ≤ .024).

Overall, the findings provide limited support for dispositional emotion regulation as an explanatory factor in healthcare-seeking and healthcare-avoidance behaviors. Future research should employ longitudinal and multimethod designs to clarify determinants of students' healthcare decision-making.

The Role of Mental Health, Resilience, and Social Engagement in Academic Performance: A Multi-Campus, Cross-Cultural Perspective
Mini Parvathi Santhosh, Anushua Biswas, Rupa Kalahasthi
Rochester Institute of Technology, Dubai

University life often includes early adulthood challenges that strain mental health through academic, social, and personal demands. Anxiety (32.1%) and depression (28.9%) are prevalent among college students worldwide, along with varying levels of stress, with risk factors such as social isolation contributing to poor mental health, while protective factors such as resilience may help students manage these challenges. Poor mental health has also been linked to declining academic performance, as difficulties with motivation, focus, and cognitive function affect students' academic performance, thus resulting in a cyclic relationship where mental health and academic performance are closely connected. Findings on this relationship remain inconsistent across cultural contexts: mental health symptoms did not significantly predict academic performance among Spanish students, while studies from students in Canada and Japan found strong predictive effects. This inconsistency suggests that cultural and institutional context may shape how mental health relates to academic performance. This study examines how mental health affects academic performance among university students across global campuses and tests whether social engagement and resilience moderate this association, using a quantitative, correlation survey-based design, thus offering insight from a multi-campus, cross-cultural perspective on how these factors shape student wellbeing across culturally diverse populations.
